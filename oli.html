1. Key Word Transformations ▶

📘 Rules to study before the exercises

Task format: Complete the second sentence so that it has a similar meaning to the first, using the word given. Do not change the given word. Use 3–5 words. No contractions. Mind grammar and spelling.

Key structures you must know:

  • Passive Voice: be + V3 — e.g. They built it → It was built.
  • Reported Speech: tense shift; say → said; will → would; time/place changes.
  • Conditionals: 1st, 2nd, 3rd, mixed; inversions: Had I known...; Were I you...
  • Gerund vs Infinitive: enjoy doing, want to do, stop doing/to do.
  • Modals & Perfect Modals: must have done, should have done, needn't have done.
  • Causative: have/get something done.
  • Phrasal verbs & idioms: make away with, cut down on, get me down, let down.
  • Inversion: Never have I..., No sooner had..., Only then did...
  • Wishes: wish + Past Simple / Past Perfect.
  • Linking words: despite / in spite of + noun/-ing; although + clause.

2. Word Formation ▶

📘 Rules to study before the exercises

Task format: Read the text. Use the word given in CAPITALS to form a new word that fits in the space in the same line.

Suffixes you must know:

  • Noun suffixes: -ness (happiness), -ment (development), -tion/-sion (decision), -ity (reality), -ance/-ence (difference), -ship (friendship), -hood (childhood), -dom (freedom), -ist (artist), -er/-or (teacher/actor).
  • Adjective suffixes: -ful (careful), -less (careless), -ous (dangerous), -ive (creative), -able/-ible (readable), -al (musical), -ic (academic), -ish (childish), -y (rainy).
  • Verb suffixes: -ise/-ize (modernise), -ify (simplify), -en (strengthen).
  • Adverb suffix: -ly (quickly).
  • Negative prefixes: un- (unhappy), in- (incorrect), im- (impossible), il- (illegal), ir- (irregular), dis- (disagree), mis- (misunderstand), non- (non-fiction).

3. Extra Word (Line Correction) ▶

📘 Rules to study before the exercises

Task format: Read the text. Some lines are correct (put V). Some lines have a word which should not be there — write that word.

Common extra words to look for:

  • Extra prepositions: e.g. discuss about, enter into, married with.
  • Extra articles: e.g. the before names, a/an with uncountable nouns.
  • Extra auxiliary verbs: e.g. do in affirmative sentences, will after if.
  • Extra pronouns: e.g. my own, your own when redundant.
  • Redundant adverbs: e.g. very with strong adjectives, far before too.
  • Words that are never used together: e.g. although ... but, because ... so.

4. Reading: Gap Fill (A–K, one extra) ▶

📘 Strategy before the exercises

Task format: Read the passage and choose which of the sentences A–K fit into the numbered gaps. There is one extra sentence which does not fit.

Tips:

  • Read the whole text first to understand the topic and flow.
  • Look for linking words (however, therefore, moreover) and pronouns (this, these, it) that connect sentences.
  • Check grammatical consistency (tenses, singular/plural).
  • Eliminate the extra sentence by confirming it doesn’t fit any gap.

5. Reading: Which Part Is It Mentioned? ▶

📘 Strategy before the exercises

Task format: Read the text divided into paragraphs (A–H). In which part is the following mentioned? Write the letter of the paragraph.

Tips:

  • Skim the text first to get the general idea of each paragraph.
  • Underline keywords in the statements, then scan for synonyms in the text.
  • Pay attention to paraphrases — the answer is rarely word-for-word.
  • Some paragraphs may be used more than once, but usually each statement matches one paragraph.

📘 Strategy before the exercises

Task format: Are the statements True (A), False (B), or Not Given (C) according to the text?

Definitions:

Exercise 7.1 — Learning about the science of shyness
On the day I received an offer from my first-choice university, I expected to feel elation. Yet when it came, I felt only dread at the thought of freshers' week and all the social challenges. I assumed this was a one-off, though the potential for rejection seemed immense. But I was wrong.

Today, as a science writer, I've witnessed a wealth of new research that has identified the psychological barriers that prevent us from building better relationships. Consider a phenomenon known as the liking gap. In a series of experiments, psychologists asked two strangers to meet and chat. Before speaking, both assumed that the exchange would feel uncomfortable — yet they ended up enjoying it far more than they had believed they would. One study examined the impressions of university dorm-mates over several months. The researchers found the liking gap, firmly established on the first meeting, persisted for several months.

When I first learned about this research, I couldn't help but cringe at all the times I had been afflicted by the liking gap at university. After meeting charming, funny and kind people, whom I wanted to get to know better, I would doubt that this was reciprocated. As a result, I would avoid any further contact for fear that I might seem embarrassingly needy.

I believe the liking gap has its roots in too much introspection. We are so busy worrying about the impression we've given that we miss all the positive signals. Digging into the scientific literature, I soon found examples of many other misplaced fears. For instance, many of us are too shy about giving compliments. We doubt our abilities to express those compliments elegantly. Expressing appreciation of others not only brings joy to the person receiving the praise but also to the person saying it. Unfortunately, our natural reserve means that most of us miss out on these opportunities to cement our bonds.

Surprisingly, I have found that simply learning about concepts such as the liking gap can help us conquer our shyness, but it also needs perseverance. For me, an ambition to speak Italian gave me the necessary push. As my fluency increased, I started arranging "conversation exchanges" with native speakers. I made many blunders, but I was touched to see how sensitively my partners saved me from embarrassment. Striking up new conversations in my native language suddenly felt a lot less daunting. At the height of my shyness at university, it would have seemed inconceivable that I would write a book about social connection. I don't regret those years of anxiety. I remember that time with overwhelming gratitude to all the people who proved my fears of rejection to be false.
